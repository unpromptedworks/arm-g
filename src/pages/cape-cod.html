Six lights, one coast, no two alike.

A light you can mistake for its neighbour is worse than no light: it tells you, with confidence, that you are somewhere else. Every characteristic is chosen against the lights around it. The outer arm of Cape Cod, with six lighthouses between Chatham at the elbow and Provincetown at the tip, shows how that is done, and has done it in two different ways.

Before flashes, count the towers

Here a station was first told apart by how many lights it showed. Cape Cod Light at Truro, now called Highland, had one tower. Chatham, lit in 1808, had two. When Congress commissioned a light for Eastham, between them, in 1837, it got three towers, the Three Sisters of Nauset, because one and two were taken.

It was a grammar of space instead of time: you read the station by counting points of light along the horizon, not by counting flashes. Every extra tower was an extra lamp to keep, and Cape Cod's cliffs kept falling into the sea underneath them. The Three Sisters were decommissioned in 1911, leaving only the middle tower lit. In 1923 Chatham's northern tower was taken down, moved about twelve miles north and put up again at Eastham as Nauset Light. That move ended both the twins and the sisters.

After: the same counts, in time

Today each of the three is a single tower, and each says its name with a rhythm. Highland flashes once. Chatham flashes twice. And the count of three was carried over on purpose: when the Sisters were reduced to the one middle tower in 1911, that light was given a triple flash, in homage, as Wikipedia's account has it, to the two sisters that had gone dark. Nauset kept three white flashes until 1981, when its lens was replaced by rotating aerobeacons and its signal became one white flash and one red. Highland's one and Chatham's two still stand: the counts the towers once spelled out in space, now said in time.

Nauset's red is what separates it from Chatham, about twelve miles south, which shares its period. Read Nauset carefully: the 10 seconds is the whole cycle, white flash and red flash together, so a flash of some colour comes every five seconds. The National Park Service describes it as one red flash and one white, with five seconds between them. Its two colours are charted to different ranges, 24 miles for white and 20 for red, so near the edge of its range you would see only the white flash, once every ten seconds: a different-looking light from the same tower.

At the tip, where the timer fails

This site argues that you should time a light first. Provincetown is where that advice runs out. Race Point and Wood End, both near the tip of the hook, both flash once every ten seconds. Your stopwatch reads the same at both. Only the colour differs, white against red, so here the colour does the work the period does elsewhere. Long Point, at the very end, takes no chances: it is green and occulting, the only one of the six that is lit for longer than it is dark.

The timer does earn its keep along the outer shore. Highland and Race Point are the two white single flashes on this coast, Fl W both, and only their periods, 5 and 10 seconds, tell them apart. A navigator rounding the Cape from the south who sees a white light flash once must time it before saying which one it is.

Read down the middle column and one number repeats: four of the six lights, Chatham, Nauset, Race Point and Wood End, repeat every ten seconds. On this coast the period separates only Highland and Long Point from the rest. For the other four, the flash count and the colour do all the work, and a reader who only times lights would be lost among them. Time first still holds as the first thing to do. Here it is not the last.
