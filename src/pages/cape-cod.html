Six lights, one coast, no two alike.

A light you can mistake for its neighbour is worse than no light: it tells you, with confidence, that you are somewhere else. On the outer arm of Cape Cod, where six lighthouses stand between Chatham at the elbow and Provincetown at the tip, stations were told apart in two ways at once for about a century, by counting towers and by timing a lamp, until timing won.

Two grammars, side by side

One way was spatial. Cape Cod Light at Truro, now called Highland, had one tower. Chatham, lit in 1808, had two. When a light was built at Eastham, between them, in 1837, it got three towers, the Three Sisters of Nauset. An inspector's report of 1838 guessed why: "They were, doubtless, given this triple appearance to distinguish them from Chatham lights." You read such a station by counting points of light along the horizon.

The other way was already in use. Race Point, near the tip of the hook, was first lit on 5 November 1816 with one of the earliest rotating beacons, which set it apart from the other lights on the Cape, Highland's steady one among them. That is a grammar of time: one point of light, read by whether it comes and goes.

How each one failed

Time failed first, and it failed by making Race Point look like its neighbour. The light was meant to revolve, but the keeper and the masters who used Boston Bay said it never went wholly dark, and in thick vapour a light that never vanishes is simply a light. In the winter of 1842 three vessels ran ashore near Race Point, each having taken it for Highland. On the night of 31 January 1845 the schooner Saluda did the same. Days later the Public Ledger reported the same failure, and another loss, in one sentence: the light "did not revolve as usual during the severe storm of Tuesday, and was therefore mistaken for another light (the Highland) in the vicinity."

Space failed more slowly. Every extra tower was an extra lamp to keep, and the cliffs kept falling into the sea beneath them. In 1911, with the northern Sister about eight feet from the edge, government tests showed that the three lights could be replaced by one flashing light, and the middle tower alone was lit. Not everyone agreed: one complaint, printed in the Boston Globe, was that "in thick weather, it could be impossible to count the flashes correctly." Both grammars break in the same weather; the coast chose the one that costs a single lamp. In 1923 Chatham's northern tower was taken down, moved about twelve miles north and put up again at Eastham as Nauset Light. That ended both the twins and the sisters.

After: the same counts, in time

Today each of the old tower stations is a single tower, and each says its name with a rhythm. Highland flashes once. Chatham flashes twice. And the count of three was carried over: the light lit in 1911 was given a triple flash, in homage, as Wikipedia's account has it, to the two sisters that had gone dark. Nauset kept three white flashes until 1981, when its lens was replaced by rotating aerobeacons and its signal became one white flash and one red. Highland's one and Chatham's two still stand: counts once spelled out in towers, now said in flashes.

Nauset and Chatham, about twelve miles apart, share a period, so the period cannot separate them. Their rhythm can. Chatham's two white flashes come close together, then a long dark. Nauset's flashes are evenly spaced, one every five seconds, and they alternate white and red. The 10 seconds is the whole cycle, white flash and red flash together; the National Park Service describes one red flash and one white, with five seconds between them.

The two colours are charted to different ranges, 24 miles for white and 20 for red. Between those distances the red flash is gone and Nauset shows one white flash every ten seconds: Fl W 10s, exactly what Race Point shows at the tip of the hook. The notation on the chart is a promise made within the charted range; near the edge of it, a light may say something else.

At the tip, where the timer fails

This site argues that you should time a light first. Provincetown is where that advice runs out. Race Point and Wood End, both near the tip of the hook, both flash once every ten seconds. Your stopwatch reads the same at both. Only the colour differs, white against red, so here the colour does the work the period does elsewhere. Long Point, at the very end, differs in everything: it is green and occulting, the only one of the six that is lit for longer than it is dark.

Along the outer shore the timer still matters. Highland and Race Point are the two white single flashes on this coast, Fl W both, and only their periods, 5 and 10 seconds, tell them apart. It is the confusion of 1842 and 1845 in modern form, now settled by a stopwatch instead of by whether the light turns: a navigator rounding the Cape who sees a white light flash once must time it before saying which one it is.

Read down the middle column and one number repeats: four of the six lights, Chatham, Nauset, Race Point and Wood End, repeat every ten seconds. On this coast the period separates only Highland and Long Point from the rest. For the other four, the flash count and the colour do all the work, and a reader who only times lights would be lost among them. Time first still holds as the first thing to do. Here it is not the last.
