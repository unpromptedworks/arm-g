A dash is only a dash beside a dot.

Every other rhythm in the grammar is counted: one flash, two, three, a group, a pause. A Morse light is the exception. Its flashes come in two lengths, and you read it by comparing them. The rule the notation gives is only that the two lengths be "clearly different". That one rule decides which letters are worth flashing at all.

The U of the North Sea

Every offshore installation in UK waters shows the same light. The Standard Marking Schedule that the UK government attaches to each installation's consent (DECC 04/11) requires a flashing white main light "to exhibit Morse letter 'U' every 15 seconds", with the parts in exact proportion: each dot as long as the dark between dots, the dash three dots long, and between one U and the next an eclipse of not less than 8 and not more than 12 seconds. The schedule does not fix the length of a dot. Those limits leave it between about 0.43 and 1 second; the player above uses half a second, so the letter takes 3.5 seconds and the dark 11.5.

The same letter is everywhere on the structure. Red lights mark its other corners, with "the same characteristics as the main light(s)", flashing in unison with each other but not necessarily with the white. The standby light that takes over if the main one fails also shows U, every 15 seconds. The schedule covers every structure or vessel it is attached to, and they all say one thing: the letter names a class of object, not one installation.

In the International Code of Signals, U sent alone means "You are running into danger." The schedule does not say that is why U was chosen, and this site has found no source that does. It is a fit, at least: a fixed steel structure in open sea is the danger.

The horn says it more slowly

When visibility falls to two miles or less, the platform sounds U on its fog signal as well, and the schedule gives every interval to the hundredth of a second: blast 0.75, silence 1.00, blast 0.75, silence 1.00, blast 2.50, then 24 seconds of silence, 30 seconds in all. Set it against the light on one axis and two things show. The horn speaks once for every two times the light does. And its proportions are not the light's: the dash is a little over three times a dot, and every gap is longer than the dot before it.

No rule says how long a dash is

Wikipedia's table of characteristics breaks down its example, Mo(K) G 6s, flash by flash: dash 1.0 second, dark 0.25, dot 0.5, dark 0.25, dash 1.0, then 3 seconds of dark. That dash is twice its dot, not three times, and the gaps inside the letter are half a dot. A telegraphist would call it badly sent. The light list does not care, because the definition asks only for "two clearly different durations". Play K beside the platform's U, at the top of the page: both are good Morse by the grammar of lights, and neither is good Morse by the grammar of the key.

What this means for you, on deck: do not try to time a dot. Compare. The first flash of a Morse light is a question, and the second is the answer: same length, or longer, or shorter. The period still tells you the light repeats, but the letter is in the ratio.

Safe water says A

A safe-water mark, which usually means deep, open water lies ahead, may show Mo(A), a short flash and a long one, or one of three other white rhythms: isophase, occulting, or a long flash every ten seconds. A is one of the two shortest Morse letters with both lengths in it (N, a dash and a dot, is the other), so it is about the briefest sentence in which a dash can be told from a dot.

If you hoped the letters followed the Code of Signals, A ends the hope. Flown or flashed between ships, A means "I have a diver down; keep well clear at slow speed", which is nothing a safe-water buoy would want to say. U on the platforms is a fit; A at sea is not. The letters on lights are a code of their own.

Letters that are not Morse at all

Now take a letter with only one length in it. The lighthouse at Gabicce Mare, on Italy's Adriatic coast, is listed as Mo(O) W 14s: three long white flashes every 14 seconds, the O of Morse. But with no dot beside it, a dash is just a flash. Nothing in the light itself says "dash", and a watcher who did not have the list would write down a group of three. If each flash lasts two seconds or more, the grammar already has a word for it: LFl(3), three long flashes.

Nine of the thirty-six Morse characters are like O, built of one length only. Every one of them has a group-flash twin, and to the eye at sea the twins are the same light.

Here is where this site takes a side. A Morse letter on a light only says something that a group cannot when it mixes the two lengths: A, U, K, D, and the rest. The other nine are group flashes written in a different alphabet, and a reader who meets Mo(O) on a chart should look for three equal flashes and not for anything Morse. Gabicce Mare may have its reasons for O; the light list, read through the grammar, cannot show them to you.
