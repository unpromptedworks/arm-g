The grammar

A characteristic is a sentence in a fixed order: how the light moves, what colour it is, how long before it repeats, how high it stands and how far it reaches. Most of the time only the first three matter to you.

Fl(2)rhythm Wcolour 10speriod 15mheight 18Mrange

Rhythm is the pattern of light and dark, with a group count in brackets. Colour is W, R, G, Y or Bu; when no colour is written, the light is white. Period is the time for the whole pattern to run once. Height is the light's elevation in metres (in US light lists, feet). Range is in nautical miles, a capital M: how far the chart says it carries. Sources for each rule are at the foot of the page.

Lit less than dark: flashing

In a flashing light, the light is on for less time, in total, than it is off. A long flash lasts two seconds or more. Flashes can come in groups, and a group can be composite: Fl(2+1) is two flashes, a pause, one flash, then the long dark. The gap between groups is at least three times the gap inside one, which is how the eye sorts a run of flashes into words.

Lit more than dark: occulting and isophase

Turn a flashing light inside out and you have an occulting one: on for longer, in total, than it is off, so what you notice are the eclipses. Groups work the same way. An isophase light is the balance point between the two: light and dark of exactly equal length.

Too fast to count: quick, very quick, ultra quick

Past a certain speed, flashes stop being things you count and become a texture. The Canadian Hydrographic Service's Chart 1 sets the bands by flashes a minute: quick, 50 to 79 (usually 50 or 60); very quick, 80 to 159 (usually 100 or 120); ultra quick, 160 or more. Here they are drawn at 60, 120 and 240. Written alone, Q runs without a break; with a bracket and a period, it comes in groups; with an I in front, its run of flashes is broken by a regular dark pause.

Spelled out: Morse

Mo(A) flashes the letter A: a short flash and a long one. Safe-water marks, which usually mean open, deep water lies ahead, often use it. Morse is the one rhythm read by comparing lengths rather than counting: Morse lights, read by length.

Several colours: alternating and sectored

Two colours written together mean one of two things. With Al in front, the light changes colour as it runs, so everyone sees both colours. Without it, the colours are sectors: the lantern shows a different colour in different directions, and the colour you see tells you which arc you are standing in. A worked set: the cardinal marks

A cardinal mark is a buoy or beacon that tells you which side of a hazard to pass: a north mark has the safe water to its north. Their lights are white, quick or very quick, and they count like a clock face. East is three flashes, as three o'clock is; south is six; west is nine. North flashes without stopping. South adds one long flash to help tell it from west in poor conditions. Each comes in a quick and a very quick version, so two marks near each other can differ.
