The one light that says "new".

Every other characteristic on a chart is a name, and names are meant to last. The emergency wreck marking buoy says something else: this danger is not on your chart yet. It is blue and yellow because no other light was, and it is taken away once the charts catch up. It was added to the grammar after one wreck in the Dover Strait was struck twice through the marks that were already guarding it.

Three weeks off Dunkirk

Early on 14 December 2002 the car carrier Tricolor, bound from Zeebrugge to Southampton, collided with the container ship Kariba, capsized, and sank about 17 nautical miles off the French coast near Dunkirk. No one died. The wreck was guarded by two warships and salvage vessels and marked with three wreck buoys.

Within two days (the next night in one account, two days later in Trinity House's) the Dutch vessel Nicola ran onto the wreck, despite the radio warnings, the guard ships and a lighted buoy, and had to be towed free. Two more patrol ships and six more buoys went out, one with a racon. On 1 January 2003 the Turkish tanker Vicky, carrying 77,000 tons of gas oil, struck the Tricolor a third time.

The marks around the wreck were not wrong. They were in the grammar a mariner already knew, and that grammar has no tense: a buoy that shows two white flashes says "isolated danger" the same way on its first night as in its twentieth year. This site's reading of the failure: an officer whose chart shows clear water sees a light and looks for it on the chart, and a light in the old grammar gives no hint that it will not be there. IALA's answer was a mark whose whole message is that the chart is out of date. Trinity House, the lighthouse authority for England and Wales, describes the result as a buoy built for "high visual and radio aid to navigation recognition".

Blue, because no light was blue

The colours of lights at sea were all spoken for. White, red and green carry the lateral marks, the cardinals, isolated danger and safe water. Yellow is the special mark's colour: a special mark's light is "always yellow", and may use any rhythm not already taken by the cardinal, isolated danger and safe-water marks. A new wreck could not borrow any of them without saying something it did not mean.

Blue was free: no other navigation mark uses it. But blue alone was not. Ashore and on the water, a blue light means police, coastguard or an ambulance, and Trinity House notes that the wreck mark's characteristic "was chosen to eliminate confusion with blue lights" used by law enforcement, security and emergency services. So the blue alternates with yellow, one second each, with half a second of dark between. The colours say "new danger" together, and neither one says it alone.

The body says the same thing by day: a pillar or spar painted in equal vertical stripes of blue and yellow, four to eight of them, and if it has a topmark, an upright yellow cross. The special mark's topmark is a yellow cross as well, turned on its side: an X. The wreck mark stands the special mark's cross up.

Called flashing, lit two-thirds of the time

Trinity House calls it "an alternating blue and yellow flashing light". Wikipedia's article on the buoy writes it as occulting. Both give the same timings, and the timings decide it. In each three seconds the light shows blue for one second and yellow for one second, and is dark for two half-seconds: two seconds of light, one of dark. Chart 1 defines a flashing light as one whose "total duration of light" is "shorter than total duration of darkness", and an occulting light as one where the light lasts longer. By the definitions on the chart, this is not a flashing light.

This site writes it Al.Oc BuY 3s, alternating occulting, blue and yellow, three seconds, and draws it with the listed lengths. The difference is not pedantry. A flashing light reads as darkness broken by light; this one reads as light broken by darkness, a buoy that is almost always lit and keeps changing its mind about its colour. If you time the listed light with this site's timer, holding while it is lit of either colour, it reads as occulting: lit two-thirds of the time is past the timer's line for occulting. The timer cannot tell blue from yellow, so it will see one rhythm at 1.5 seconds, not a cycle of three. That is the honest reading of the rhythm. The colour is what makes it a wreck.

When several wreck buoys are laid around one wreck, their lights are meant to be synchronised. A pattern of buoys around a hull then blinks as one light, and the eye takes in the shape of the danger, not a scatter of separate marks.

The one light with an end date

Every other light on a chart is meant to stay until someone decides to change it. The wreck mark is meant to go. It is expected for the first 24 to 72 hours after a wreck; then permanent marks, isolated danger or cardinal, should take over and the charts be updated. Trinity House keeps it in place until the wreck is well known and published in nautical publications, fully surveyed for position and least depth, and permanently marked.

So one wreck speaks three sentences in its life. First, Al.Oc BuY: new, not on your chart. Then Fl(2) W on a single isolated danger mark, or the quick white counts of the cardinal marks around it: known, on your chart, pass on this side. Finally, if the wreck is cleared, nothing at all.

On radar it says D

The buoy may also carry a racon, a radar beacon that paints a code on a ship's radar screen, and the code suggested for a wreck mark is Morse D: a dash and two dots. By the rule on the Morse page, D is a real Morse letter, one that mixes both lengths and could not be mistaken for a group. The light says "new" in colour, the radar says "danger" in Morse, and neither depends on the other.
