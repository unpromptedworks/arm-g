Three lights a stopwatch can't tell apart.

Everywhere else on this site the rule is time first, count second. The cardinal marks are where that rule breaks. Their periods are shared so freely that a watch can't tell east from west, and IALA says you never need to know how fast they flash. What tells them apart is the shape of each burst, and one long flash at the end of the south mark's.

Ten seconds, three directions

A cardinal mark stands on one side of a danger and tells you to pass on that side: keep north of a north mark. Every one of them shows a white light, and each comes in two versions, quick (60 flashes a minute) and very quick (120). IALA's recommendation on the rhythms of lights, E-110, fixes the period of each version. East: 10 seconds quick, 5 very quick. South and west: 15 quick, 10 very quick.

Read those numbers as a watcher with a stopwatch would. The three lights above all repeat every 10 seconds, and they stand on three different sides of their dangers. East and west send you opposite ways. At either rate, south and west always share a period. The only cardinal a watch can name on its own is north, which has no period at all: it flashes without stopping.

IALA's note on the marks says why this doesn't matter to them: identifying a cardinal mark "does not require knowledge of which of the two rates is being shown", and when two marks of the same kind are near each other, their periods will differ. On a cardinal, the period is how one east mark is told from the next east mark. It is not how you learn the mark is east. That is the count's job.

Six against nine

E-110 gives example timings for each cardinal, and the players on this page use them: at the quick rate, half a second of light and half a second of dark; at the very quick rate, a quarter and a quarter. A quick light is a clock. Each flash and the dark after it take one second, so counting a quick cardinal is counting seconds.

Now set south and west at the quick rate side by side. Both repeat every 15 seconds. The west's nine flashes run 8.5 seconds from the first flash to the last. The south's six flashes and its long flash run 8 seconds. Same period, nearly the same length of burst, nearly the same length of dark after it. If you lose count somewhere around the fifth flash, they are one light, except at the very end.

The long flash is that end. Wikipedia says the south mark "may be augmented" with one, as if it were optional; in E-110 it is part of the character, a long flash "of not less than 2 s duration" after the six. E-110 also fixes where it goes. The dark before the long flash must equal the dark between the quick flashes, so it is not a separate word: it is a seventh flash that stays lit. And it must be no longer than the dark that follows it, so it can't run into the next group. Trinity House says it is there "to make it more distinctive"; Wikipedia, to tell south from west "in difficult conditions".

Read the shape, not the number

Put the four quick marks on one 15-second axis and the count drops out. North never stops. East is a short burst, and three flashes are few enough to hear as a rhythm without counting them. South is a long burst that ends on a held light. West is a long burst that ends on a short flash, then dark. That is four shapes, and you can tell them apart having counted nothing past three.

This is the site's reading, not IALA's, and the site is willing to be wrong about it: the long flash matters most to the watcher who has already lost count. Someone who counts six correctly doesn't need it. Someone who loses count at five, in a swell, with spray on the glass, still knows which mark it is, from how the burst ends. So the order for a cardinal is shape first, count second, and time last, to tell this east mark from another one on the chart.

The very quick versions keep the shapes but squeeze them. A very quick west's nine flashes take 4.25 seconds, not far off a quick east's three at 2.5. That is why "a long burst or a short one" isn't enough on its own when the two rates sit in the same water: three is still few enough to hear as a rhythm, and nine is not.

What the timer will tell you

The site's timer reads a period first and a count second, the order this page argues against. Fed E-110's timings for the five cardinals that have groups, with every press up to 0.06 seconds early or late, it wrote each one back correctly in the site's tests. The trouble is a missed press. Drop the fifth flash of each round and the quick west comes out Fl(4+4) 15s and the quick south Fl(4+2) 15s: the timer reads the missing flash as a break between two groups, and loses the south's long flash with it. Neither reading is a cardinal at all, which is at least a warning. On a cardinal, watch how the burst ends before you trust a count, the timer's or your own.
