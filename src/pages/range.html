Distance edits every word of a light but one.

Chatham and Cape Hatteras are both charted to carry 24 miles. From the deck of a small boat you will see Hatteras about 20 miles off and Chatham about 14, because a range on the chart is a promise about the lamp, not about the horizon. Before a light reaches you, distance and weather can drop its flashes, shorten them and change its colour. They can't change its clock.

Correction: the ranges on this page for Chatham (24M), Highland (18M) and Race Point (16M) come from Wikipedia. The Coast Guard's Light List of 25 November 2026 gives all three 14 miles, so Chatham's lamp and its horizon nearly agree, and the gap this page opens with belongs to Hatteras and Fastnet alone. The horizons and the argument about the period stand; the drawings will be redone.

Three ranges, and the shortest wins

The number with the capital M is the nominal range. The US Coast Guard defines it as how far a light can be seen when the meteorological visibility is exactly 10 nautical miles. It is a measure of the lamp's strength, and it is the only range a light list prints. Two other ranges decide what you actually see.

The luminous range is the nominal range corrected for tonight's weather. In thinner air the light carries farther than its M; in haze it carries less. The Light List prints a diagram for reading one from the other, and it warns that the answers are approximate.

The geographic range is the horizon. It depends on two heights, the light's and your eye's, and not at all on the lamp. The Light List's table uses the rule that the horizon from a height of h feet is 1.17 times the square root of h nautical miles. You add the light's distance and your own. From an eye 10 feet above the water, your share is 3.7 miles.

You see a light at the shorter of its luminous and geographic ranges. The figure below sorts eight of this site's lights by their M, longest first. Draw in the horizon and the order changes: Fastnet, charted farthest, is seen less far than Cape Byron or Hatteras, and Chatham, tied with Hatteras on the chart, is seen 5.6 miles less far.

Read the hatched bars. Fastnet's lamp is charted to carry 28 miles and its lantern is 49 metres up, but from a 10-foot eye the rock is under the horizon until you are 18.5 miles off. Chatham is the extreme case on this site: a 24-mile lamp in a lantern 24 metres up, so ten of its charted miles are hidden. Only two lights here are limited by their lamps: Highland, high on its cliff, and Long Point, a short harbour light whose 8 miles fall inside a horizon it could reach. Cape Byron, 118 metres up, has a horizon and a lamp that almost exactly agree.

A light below the horizon is not always invisible. The Light List notes that the "loom" of a powerful light, its glow on the sky, is often seen beyond the reach of its direct rays, sometimes sharp enough to take a bearing. A loom comes and goes in time with the light, so it can be timed before the light itself is up. Its flashes and colour you can't trust yet.

What distance does to each word

The Light List's own notes on "conditions which may affect a light" read, taken together, as a list of edits. Set them against the parts of a characteristic and every word but one is on it.

WordWhat distance and weather can do to it
RhythmOn a boat pitching in a heavy sea, a light on the horizon "may alternatively appear and disappear", and the Light List warns this can lead the unwary to "assign a false characteristic". A pair of flashes with one lost behind a wave reads as one. Close in, the opposite: some flashing lights show "a faint continuous light between flashes", and read as fixed and flashing.
Flash length"Haze and distance may reduce the apparent duration of the flash." A long flash can fade toward an ordinary one at the edge of range.
Colour"In some atmospheric conditions, white lights may have a reddish hue." Ice or snow on the lantern can make coloured lights look white. And a light's colours need not carry equally far: Nauset's red is charted to 20 miles and its white to 24, so between the two Nauset says Fl W 10s. You need height to see it: Nauset's lantern is 37 metres up, so only an eye about 50 feet up, a ship's bridge, has a horizon past 20 miles (about 21).
Height, rangeNeither tells you how far off you are. "The distance of an observer from a light cannot be estimated by its apparent intensity": a powerful light far off and a buoy close by can look alike.
PeriodNothing. Every flash that reaches you still falls on the light's clock. A flash lost to a wave is missing, not moved, so the time from a flash to the same flash in a later round is the period or a whole multiple of it.

This is the strongest case for the site's rule, time first. At the edge of range the count can't be trusted, the flash length can't be trusted and the colour can't be trusted. The period can, as long as you take the shortest of several timings and not the first one. The Light List's remedy for a false characteristic is to close the distance or climb higher. Timing is what you can do while you wait.

Where the site may be wrong: a group light at the horizon can lose the same flash every round. If the swell keeps hiding the second flash of Fl(2) W 10s, what you time is Fl W 10s, with the right period and the wrong count, and Chatham reads as Race Point. The period survives, but on its own it doesn't name the light.

A shorter flash carries less far

Flash length costs range even in clear air. The eye does not see a short flash at the lantern's full intensity. The Blondel–Rey law, an empirical rule from 1911 that IALA still uses, gives a flash of t seconds an effective intensity of t / (a + t) of the same lamp burning steadily, where a is a constant. Countries have used values from 0.1 to 0.3 seconds; 0.2 is the classic one. A 0.1-second flash then counts as a third of the lamp, and a one-second flash as five-sixths.

A third of the light is not a third of the range, because haze, not distance, does most of the dimming. The three players below are one lamp, which burning steadily would carry 10 miles, flashing once every 5 seconds with three flash lengths, each drawn at its exact length. The ranges under them are the site's arithmetic, with a = 0.2 and IALA's definition of nominal range (10 miles of visibility, and IALA's night threshold of 0.2 microlux at the eye).

The same grammar, the same period, and 1.7 miles between the first and the last, about a sixth of the lamp's reach. With the 0.1-second constant that IALA's engineers now favour for marine lights, the gap closes to about a mile (8.7 against 9.8). You never do this sum at sea, since the light list prints the range, not the lamp. It is a reason to read a short flash at the edge of range as the first thing that will go.
